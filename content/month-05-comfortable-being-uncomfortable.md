## Safety first (read this before anything else)

This month is about small, safe, chosen discomforts. It is never about danger, pain or pushing through a real problem.

- **No fasting is expected of Monty (6) or Rudy (8).** For Duke and Indiana, any fasting is optional, light, and **your decision as the parent**. Follow Church guidance and your own judgment. This guide does not prescribe any fast.
- **No prolonged cold-water or heat exposure for the younger two.** For Duke and Indiana, any cold exposure is **optional** and limited to a **short cold finish on a normal shower**, then warm up. No breath-holding, no ice baths, no cold water swimming. Parents decide.
- **Hikes and long walks** need a weather check, the right clothing, water, a known route and an adult plan (who, where, when back, a charged phone, a way to cut it short).
- **Never push through pain, dizziness, illness or injury.** Stopping is allowed and is not failing.
- **No sleep deprivation.** "No snooze button" means getting up at the planned time, not sleeping less.
- If any child has a health concern (asthma, heart, joints, anything), **check with your doctor** before adding exercise or exposure.
- Scale everything by age. Each child sets their own level **inside** the safe range you set.

## 1. The Big Idea

**Principle:** *Discomfort is training, not danger. When I choose small, safe hard things on purpose, I find out I can handle more than I thought.*

Modern life is built to remove effort: lifts, cars, delivery, heated rooms, instant everything. Some of that is wonderful. But if I never feel a little hard, I never get the proof that I can do hard things, and then ordinary hard things (a tough class, a mistake, a scary new person) feel bigger than they are. This month each kid picks small, safe discomforts, keeps a log of what they expected versus what actually happened, and finishes with a real long walk.

**Four ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **Hard is not the same as harmful.** My brain says "stop" long before my body needs to. I can notice that, and decide.
2. **Choose it on purpose.** A hard thing I pick myself feels different from one that is done to me.
3. **Expected versus actual.** I guess how bad it will be, then check. It is usually less bad than the guess. That gap is the lesson.
4. **Carry it.** Strength is built by carrying something a little heavier, a little farther, a little longer, a little at a time.

- *"All these things shall give thee experience":* D&C 122:7-8 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *Training the body, running the race:* 1 Corinthians 9:24-27 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *The heavens declare the glory of God (the outdoors):* Psalm 19:1-4 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *The Savior chose a hard thing for us:* Luke 22:42 (Gethsemane); D&C 19:16-19. **Please verify.**
- *Burdens made light:* Mosiah 24:14-15. **Please verify.**

Do not turn it into a lesson about toughness. His suffering was for us, and we are not asked to earn anything by being uncomfortable.

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 5

### Why this month matters
Kids who never feel mildly hard never collect evidence that they can handle it. Psychologist Albert Bandura's self-efficacy work (the belief "I can do this") says that **actually doing a hard thing** is the strongest source of that belief, stronger than being told you can. This month is deliberate, small, safe practice at that. It is not a toughness contest, not health advice, and not a way to "toughen up" a sensitive kid. It is a month of "I chose it, I did it, and I learned something."

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** a kid can feel scared or upset and still do the thing. Both are true. Warm, calm and firm.

**The fathering idea (The Intentional Father):** you go first. Kids learn what hard looks like from watching you do it, including the parts where you struggle.

### Kickoff (Mon Mar 1)
A short family night (20–30 minutes):
1. Explain: "This month is *Comfortable Being Uncomfortable*. Hard is not the same as dangerous. We're going to choose small, safe hard things on purpose and find out we can handle more than we thought. We're all doing it, including me."
2. **Go first:** tell them your comfort fast and one hard thing you once did that you're glad about.
3. State the **safety rules** simply: nothing dangerous, anyone can stop, we dress for the weather.
4. Introduce the **Long Walk** on Sat Mar 20 and *Shackleton's Journey*.
5. Show each kid their page in the app. Offer Indiana hers separately and lightly, as a menu.
6. Make it fun: hot chocolate, a map on the table.

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
The same principles as Months 1 and 2, with a few that matter extra this month:

- **Never use screen time as a reward or a punishment.** No "finish the walk and you get the tablet."
- **No payment per task and no promised rewards.** Don't say "if you complete the walk you get X."
- **Do** celebrate with ceremony: the badge moment, the month-end outing (everyone goes regardless).
- **Surprise delight after the fact:** "You walked in the drizzle and didn't complain. Want to grab a hot chocolate?"
- **Do** invest in real interests as support: proper walking socks or a rain jacket, a map, a pair of gloves, a book about explorers, a horse-riding lesson or a stable day for Indiana, a ski day if a trip lands here. Offer it as support, not payment.
- **Don't compare siblings.** Monty's 2 miles and Duke's 9 are different on purpose.
- **Autonomy is a reward:** let kids choose their comfort fast, their snack, their route.
- **Fading:** if a kid starts choosing hard things on their own, scale back your extras.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Be curious. Tell your own comfort fast first. Do not lecture about "kids today are soft."
- **Week 2:** Expect grumbling about the comfort fast. Say "That sounds hard. What's your plan?" and let it be. A missed day is data, not failure.
- **Week 3:** Check the weather early, and have the backup day ready. Keep your own pack heavy and real. Stay calm with complaints and stop the walk if there is any sign of real pain, illness or distress.
- **Week 4:** Holy Week. Keep it light and gentle. Reflecting matters more than doing.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** What is the hardest thing you ever did that turned out fun? What is a comfort you would miss most? What is the muddiest you have ever been?
**Medium:** Why do people choose hard things like marathons or climbing? How do you tell the difference between "this is hard" and "this is not safe"? When does your brain say "stop" too early?
**Deep:** What hard thing are you avoiding that you might actually be able to do? When have you done something hard for another person? Why do you think the Savior was willing to go through what He did for us?
**After *Cool Runnings*:** What did the team choose that was hard? What did they have to put up with? What made them keep going?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Discomfort log on the fridge** (Week 1): everyone adds one entry a week.
- **Carry It** (Week 2): carry the shopping home together.
- **The Family Walk** (Week 3): a walk at the youngest's pace, with a snack stop.
- **Walk and wonder:** a short walk in the rain with proper kit, looking for five things you would normally miss.
- **Easter week evening** (Week 4): a quiet reading, then a calm dinner.

### Your gift: do it with them (Week 3 and 4)
Do the Long Walk too. Carry a heavy pack (the heaviest, not the lightest). Do your own comfort fast and log. Name out loud where you found it hard: "My legs were burning on that hill and I wanted to stop. I slowed down and kept going." Kids need to see you struggling and continuing, not pretending it was easy. Ask them for their log entries and share yours.

### Per-kid notes
- **Duke:** Fairness matters to him, so apply every rule to yourself and explain why. If he disagrees with the plan (route, pace, rules), hear him out and negotiate inside the safety limits. He is almost 14, so his Long Walk is a real challenge. Build up with practice walks. He is comfortable in his own skin but shy with new people, so the "teach" step can be to a parent or a friend he trusts.
- **Indiana:** She is rebellious and has said she doesn't want programs like this, so keep this an invitation. She picks 3 of 5 and designs her own challenge. Her interests (horses, farm animals, skiing, art, boba with a friend) are the best material: a stable day, a long walk to a farm, a ski day. Incentives are investments in her interests, never payment. If she says no, keep the door open and invite again with no pressure.
- **Rudy:** He wants help rather than doing it himself, so the habit is "try first, then ask." On the walk, a navigation leg is the practice: look, think, guess, then ask. He is emotional and tries hard to be good, so let him cry if he needs to, and name what he did well. Game language works ("level", "boss fight").
- **Monty:** Short, physical, concrete. He is wild and independent, so give him real jobs (carry the snack, count the steps). His stretch is saying "this is hard" out loud and then continuing, which links to the hard-minute task. Don't let him sprint ahead of the group on the road. He may race ahead. Set rules about staying within sight.

### Coach, don't rescue
When they say "this is too hard," resist the instant fix. Try: *"That sounds hard. What do you think you could do?"* Then wait a moment. But **safety is not a coaching moment.** If there is pain, injury, illness, real fear or danger, stop and help immediately.

### What to watch for
- **Normal:** grumbling, complaining about the comfort fast, tired legs, a grumpy first hour.
- **Needs attention:** pain that doesn't ease, dizziness, being unusually cold or unwell, intense or prolonged distress, anxiety that grows, or any sign the child is hiding a problem to please you (especially Rudy and Duke, who both try hard to be good). Stop and talk to a doctor or someone you trust.
- **Resistance:** a "no" is information. Ask what's behind it. With Indiana, keep the invitation open.
- **Perfectionism:** if a kid is anxious about "failing" the walk, reassure them that a shortened walk is a success.

### If the month falls apart
If you reach Mar 20 with little done, do this and nothing else: **the Long Walk (shortened or on a flexible day), a few Discomfort Log entries, and the gift of doing it with them.** That's the heart of the month.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* · *Research* · *Author's argument* · *Tool*.

#### Claim: "Kids should get about an hour of active movement a day"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"Doctors in the UK say kids and teens should move around hard enough to breathe harder for about an hour a day."*
- **Source:** UK Chief Medical Officers' physical activity guidelines for ages 5–18: an average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, with activities that strengthen muscle and bone on three days a week. [GOV.UK: UK Chief Medical Officers' physical activity guidelines](https://gov.uk/government/publications/physical-activity-guidelines-uk-chief-medical-officers-report/uk-chief-medical-officers-physical-activity-guidelines). A 2026 CMO document also appeared in my search results, but I could not confirm what changed from 2019.
- **Label:** Research-based guideline (expert consensus). A long walk helps meet it, but the guideline is not a reason to push a tired or unwell child.

#### Claim: "Doing a hard thing builds the belief that you can do hard things"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"A scientist named Albert Bandura found that the best way to believe you can do something is to actually do something like it."*
- **Source:** Albert Bandura (1977), "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change," *Psychological Review*, 84(2), 191–215. He identifies mastery experiences as the strongest source of self-efficacy, with watching others, encouragement and body states as other sources. I confirmed the citation and the ranking through secondary summaries, not the paper itself.
- **Label:** Research (a theory with a lot of supporting work). It is a general theory. It does not prove that any specific activity here will work for your child.

#### Claim: "Small, safe discomforts may build resilience" (Michael Easter's argument)
- **Source:** Michael Easter, *The Comfort Crisis* (Rodale Books, 2021; 304 pages per search listing). He argues that modern comfort has weakened us, and recommends seeking out discomfort (walking, carrying, boredom, cold, and a yearly "misogi," a very hard one-day challenge). The misogi idea, as I understand it, comes from fitness coach Marcus Elliott. I did not verify that.
- **Label:** Author's argument, in the genre of popular science. My search found reader and reviewer impressions (anecdotal, selective, written from a privileged position) but **no independent check of its scientific claims**. Treat it as a stimulating idea, not as established science. Most of its evidence is about adults. **The book is not written for children, and the misogi idea is meant for adults.**

#### Claim: "Cold exposure is good for you"
- **Label:** Be cautious. I have not verified specific studies for this month, and I know of no solid evidence of health benefits for children. Here it is used only as a **self-efficacy exercise** (doing a short, safe, chosen thing that feels unpleasant), **not as health advice**. Keep it to a short cold finish on a normal shower for older kids, optional, and parents decide.

#### Claim: "The Stoic idea: focus on what you control"
- **Source:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.20 ("The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way," translations vary), via the Wisdom Map. Ryan Holiday, *The Obstacle Is the Way* (the Wisdom Map's book for this tactic).
- **Label:** Author's argument (ancient philosophy and a popular book). It is a way of thinking, not research.

#### Principle: "Connection comes before correction" and "you go first"
- **Source:** Becky Kennedy, *Good Inside*; *The Intentional Father*. **Please add the specific passages.** I am not confident enough of their contents to cite them.
- **Label:** Author's argument.
