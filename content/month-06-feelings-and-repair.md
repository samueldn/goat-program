## 1. The Big Idea

**Principle:** *Feelings are information. I can name it, feel it, and choose what I do next. When I get it wrong, I can make it right.*

Months 1 to 5 built the base. This month is about what happens when things go wrong inside us (big feelings) and between us (hurt, fights, mistakes). Kids who can name a feeling, calm their body, learn from a failure and repair a relationship have a skill that lasts all their lives. This is not about being perfect. It is about knowing the way back.

**Four ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **Name it.** Feelings are information, not enemies. Naming a feeling helps me handle it.
2. **Calm it.** My body can be calmed. I choose a tool that works for me.
3. **Failure is data.** When something doesn't work, I can ask what it taught me.
4. **Make it right.** I'm a good kid having a hard moment. I can repair what I broke. A four-step apology: **Name it, Own it, Make it right, Change it.**

**Faith thread (scriptures supporting the principles):**
- *Repair as a covenant way of life:* Mosiah 18:8-10 (from the Wisdom Map; bearing one another's burdens and mourning with those who mourn).
- *Honest correction comes from love:* Revelation 3:19 ("As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten"; from the Wisdom Map).
- *Accepting one another:* Romans 15:7 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *The Savior's victory over death, the root of hope:* Luke 24:1-6 (**please verify**).
- *A father who runs to meet his son (repentance as coming home):* Luke 15:11-32 (**please verify**). The older brother's complaint about fairness (verses 25-32) is for Duke.
- *Forgiven and remembered no more:* D&C 58:42-43 (**please verify**).
- *Repentance and relief:* Alma 36:17-20 and Enos 1:1-8 (**please verify**).
- *Make peace with someone you have wronged:* Matthew 5:23-24 (**please verify**).
- *A gentle answer:* Proverbs 15:1 (**please verify**).

The Atonement is not a reason to feel worse. Repentance means turning toward God and toward people, and it comes with hope. Use "repair" and "make it right," never shame.

## 3. The Challenge (individual)

> **"Make It Right."** Each kid does three things: (1) **tries something they expect to fail at, and debriefs what it taught them.** (2) **Makes one real repair with someone.** (3) **Teaches a calm-down tool to a parent or a friend.** The challenge day is **Sat Apr 24**, and the three parts can be spread over Weeks 3 and 4.

**Ground rules:**
- The repair can be with a parent, a friend, a teacher, a coach, or a sibling. **An apology that isn't accepted still counts.** The point is the repair attempt, not the other person's response.
- Nobody picks a failure that is unsafe or humiliating. Failure is chosen, small and recoverable.
- You are not grading how well it went.

| Kid | The failure experiment | The repair | The teach | The hard part (the stretch) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Monty (6)** | Try a physical thing he can't do yet (a cartwheel, a handstand, standing on one leg for 30 seconds) 5 times and say what his body learned | After roughhousing, apologize to someone in his own words, then make it right with an action (help clean up, a hug if wanted) | Teach one body calm-down (drum breathing, pillow squeeze, wall push) to a parent or friend | Do the apology without a parent prompting each step |
| **Rudy (8)** | Pick a hard level, shot or drawing and try 5 times. Fill in a "what each try taught me" table | A real repair using the four steps | Teach his Calm Toolkit card to a parent or friend | Use a calm-down tool on his own at a 4 or 5 feeling, **try first, then ask** |
| **Duke (13)** | Try something he expects to fail at (a harder robotics build, a new skill move). Write a short debrief | A repair for a time he was right but unkind, with a parent, friend or teammate | Teach the hand model and one calm-down tool | Ask a coach or teacher for one honest piece of feedback and act on it (Radical Candor, received) |
| **Indiana (12)** | **Her design.** Her failure, her format | **Her design.** A repair she chooses | **Her design.** A calm-down tool she shares in her way | Include one honest, no-spin conversation about something she finds hard (for example, sticking with drama) |

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 6

### Why this month matters
Every family has conflict. What matters is whether kids learn that relationships can be repaired and that feelings can be handled. This month is about two skills: understanding and calming big feelings, and making things right after you get something wrong. You are the biggest factor: kids learn repair by watching you do it.

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** "You're a good kid having a hard moment." The child isn't the problem. They are a good kid who lacks a skill right now. The limit is real and the love is real, at the same time.

**The faith idea (Covenants and Repentance):** the Atonement is repair, not shame. Easter just passed. Because of the Resurrection, no mistake is final, and turning back is always possible.

**The tactic (Radical Candor):** care personally, challenge directly. Honest correction with love and healthy limits. For you, that means naming the behavior kindly, holding the boundary, and not hiding hard truths to avoid conflict.

### Kickoff (Thu Apr 1)
A short family night (20–30 minutes). It's Easter holidays, so keep it easy.
1. Explain: "This month is *Feelings & Repair*. We'll learn names for feelings, ways to calm down, and how to make things right when we mess up. And Mom and Dad are doing it too."
2. **Go first:** tell about a time you got it wrong and made it right (or didn't).
3. Introduce the challenge (Make It Right, Sat Apr 24) and the movie.
4. Show each kid their page in the app. Offer Indiana hers separately and lightly.
5. Explain this is the end of Chapter 1 and there is a celebration for everyone at the end.

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
The same principles as Months 1 and 2, with these extra:

- **Never use screen time as a reward or a punishment.** That includes taking it away after a meltdown. A calm-down is not a consequence.
- **Never pay for tasks, and promise no rewards for apologies.** An apology you reward becomes a performance.
- **Do** celebrate with ceremony: the individual badge ceremony and the Chapter 1 celebration that everyone attends.
- **Do** invest in real interests as support, not payment: a group riding lesson trial or art supplies for Indiana, a robotics kit for Duke, a sports activity for Monty, a new board game for Rudy. Offer them when you notice effort, not as a contingent deal.
- **Surprise delight** after the fact: "I saw how you took a breath instead of yelling. Let's get a hot chocolate."
- **Don't force apologies.** A forced "sorry" teaches hiding, not repair. Invite repair when the child is calm.
- **Don't compare siblings,** and don't ask one kid to forgive another on a schedule.
- **Fading:** when a kid uses a tool on their own, step back.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Short, light week during the holidays. Name your own feelings out loud. Don't fix feelings, just label them.
- **Week 2:** Practice calm-down tools when everyone is calm, not in the middle of a meltdown. A tool isn't learned in the storm.
- **Week 3:** The apology week. Wait until the child is calm. If a kid refuses, say "Whenever you're ready" and drop it. Don't make repair a sibling negotiation: if a sibling won't receive it, it still counts.
- **Week 4:** Teach week. Let the kids be the experts. Keep your gift in mind: your repair comes first, with no expectation of a response.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** What feeling would you give a color? What do you do when you're really frustrated at a game? What's the funniest mistake you've ever made?
**Medium:** What's a feeling people try to hide? What does your body do when you're mad? What makes a good apology, and what makes a bad one? Can someone be right and still hurt someone?
**Deep:** Is it brave to say sorry? What's something you still wish you could make right? Why do you think forgiveness matters so much in our faith?
**After *Inside Out*:** Why did Sadness matter? What happened when Joy tried to run everything? What do you do with a feeling you don't like?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Feelings weather report** (Week 1): at dinner, everyone gives a weather forecast for their feeling.
- **Calm-down corner** (Week 2): a cushion, a squeeze ball, a drawing pad.
- **Oops stories** (Week 3): parents first.
- **Easter reflection** (Week 1): read Luke 24:1-6 and talk about hope.
- **Family photo or "Chapter 1" collage** (Week 4).

### Your gift: your own repair (Week 4)
Apologize to **each child** for something specific, using the four steps, with **no expectation of a response**.

1. **Name it:** "I yelled at you when you spilled the milk."
2. **Own it:** "That was my choice and it wasn't okay. You didn't deserve it."
3. **Make it right:** "What would help? I'd like to ___."
4. **Change it:** "Next time, I'll take a breath and speak calmly."

Don't add "but." Don't ask them to apologize back. If they say nothing, that's fine.

### Per-kid notes
- **Monty:** Short and physical. His fights at home and calm outside show he can regulate. This month, give him words and a body tool for home. "Strong and safe" means his strength has rules. Make the rules with him, don't impose them.
- **Rudy:** Big feelings are normal for him. The "try first, then ask" habit now applies to feelings: try the tool for two minutes, then ask for help. Never leave him alone with a feeling, because he is not meant to do this alone. He just tries first.
- **Duke:** Fairness is his compass. His disagreements are legitimate. He needs a way to disagree well rather than a rule to stop. When he was right but unkind, repair the delivery, not the point. Don't punish his strong opinions.
- **Indiana:** Offer, never require. Don't confront her about quitting. Ask with curiosity: "What did you like? What was hard?" If drama classes are the issue, consider group formats, a short course, or one performance goal. Her own pace matters. Consider offering a group riding lesson trial as an investment in her interests.

### Coach, don't rescue
When a kid is upset, resist fixing it or demanding an apology. Try: *"That sounds really hard. What do you need right now?"* Then wait. A kid who is calm can repair. A kid who is flooded cannot.

### What to watch for
- **Normal:** big feelings, tears, arguments, a sibling who won't accept an apology, a kid who resists naming feelings.
- **Needs attention:** feelings that stay intense for weeks, persistent sadness or worry, sleep or appetite changes, withdrawal from friends, talk of hopelessness, self-harm, or not wanting to go to school or activities. If you see this, talk to your pediatrician or a counselor. This guide is not therapy and is not a substitute for it.
- **Resistance:** a "no" is information. Ask what's behind it.

### If the month falls apart
Do these three things and nothing else: **name one feeling each day, teach one calm-down tool (try it once), and make your own repair with each child.** Then hold the Chapter 1 celebration anyway.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* · *Research* · *Author's argument* · *Tool*.

#### Claim: "Putting a feeling into words can calm it"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"Scientists scanned people's brains and found that when they named an emotion, the alarm part of the brain got quieter."*
- **Source:** Lieberman et al. (2007), "Putting feelings into words: affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli," *Psychological Science*, 18(5), 421-428 (PMID 17576282).
- **Label:** Research, but small. It was 30 adults looking at pictures of faces. The lead author noted the effects were modest. Don't oversell it. Say: *"Naming a feeling seems to help."*

#### Tool: "Hand model of the brain" and "Name it to tame it"
- **Source:** Dan Siegel, *The Whole-Brain Child* (with Tina Payne Bryson, 2011); the phrase "name it to tame it" is widely attributed to Siegel.
- **Label:** Tool and author's argument. It is a **teaching metaphor**, not exact anatomy. The brain does not literally "flip its lid."

#### Tool: Emotion vocabulary and zones
- **RULER** (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence, Marc Brackett): a school program built around naming and understanding emotions. A 2019 review in *Educational Psychologist* and an earlier 2012 study found positive results, but much of the research comes from the developers, and the 2012 study was described as preliminary.
- **Zones of Regulation:** a popular color-based tool. An independent 2024 review (Radzilowicz and Weiss, *Science in Autism Treatment*) concluded it **cannot yet be called evidence-based** (few studies). The developer cites more support.
- **Label:** Tool. Use for vocabulary and color-naming, not as a proven treatment.

#### Claim: "Failure is data" (growth mindset)
- **Source:** Carol Dweck's work on mindset, and the 2018 meta-analysis Sisk et al., *Psychological Science*, 29(4), 549-571.
- **Label:** Research, **disputed.** The meta-analysis found weak overall effects of growth-mindset interventions on achievement. Others argue that it was too harsh. So use "failure is data" as a useful habit, not a scientific guarantee.

#### Claim: "Repair matters more than being a perfect parent"
- **Source:** Becky Kennedy, *Good Inside* (2022), and her TED talk on repair. She describes repair as going back to a moment of disconnection, owning your part, and acknowledging the impact. "You're a good kid having a hard moment" is her broader idea of "good inside." I found the step list only in secondary summaries.
- **Label:** Author's argument (clinical perspective, written for parents). Not a controlled study.

#### Tool: The four-step apology (Name it, Own it, Make it right, Change it)
- **Source:** This is **my own kid-friendly framing** of common elements of apology and repair advice. I did **not** find it as a four-step list from a single named source, and I'm not attributing it to Kennedy or anyone else. The ingredients (acknowledge what happened, take responsibility, repair, change) match Kennedy's description of repair as summarized above.
- **Label:** Tool (the father's framing).

#### Principle: "Care personally, challenge directly" (Radical Candor)
- **Source:** Kim Scott, *Radical Candor* (2017). The two axes, Care Personally and Challenge Directly, give four quadrants: Radical Candor, Obnoxious Aggression, Ruinous Empathy and Manipulative Insincerity.
- **Label:** Author's argument. Written for workplace management, applied here to family and sports feedback.

#### Claim: "Easter 2027 is March 28"
- **Source:** most Western Easter date sources agree on Mar 28, 2027 (some sites disagree, which I treated as errors). Orthodox Pascha is May 2.
- **Label:** Fact, calculated. Check your church calendar.
