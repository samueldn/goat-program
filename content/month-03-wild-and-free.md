## 1. The Big Idea

**Principle:** *I can explore, I can handle real things on my own, and I can care for living things.*

Last month was about attention. This month is about what you do with it out in the real world. Independence is built by doing real things with real (safe) responsibility: packing your own bag, finding your own way, cooking, tying, fixing, feeding. Kids who get to explore, play outside and look after something alive practice courage, judgment and care. The month is built around the Centered Life tactics of **Connection with Nature** and **Survival Skills**, with **Embracing Hard Things** in support (a wet January walk counts).

**Four ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **Look closely.** The outdoors is full of things I walk past. When I slow down and look, I find them.
2. **Be ready.** Knowing how to pack, tie, cook and help someone who's hurt makes me calm instead of scared.
3. **Do it myself.** Real jobs, a little at a time, with a plan. Every step I take on my own earns me more freedom.
4. **Care for something alive.** An animal or a plant depends on me. Looking after it every day makes me kind and dependable.

**Faith thread (scriptures supporting the principles):**
- *The heavens and the earth declare God's glory:* Psalm 19:1–4 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *Be prepared and you need not fear:* D&C 38:30 (from the Wisdom Map).
- *Hard things can be for our good:* D&C 122:7–8 (from the Wisdom Map; supports "Embracing Hard Things").
- *Stewardship over creation (please verify):* Genesis 1:26–31; Genesis 2:15 ("to dress it and to keep it"); Moses 3:15.
- *Care for animals (please verify):* Proverbs 12:10 ("A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast"); Matthew 6:26 (the birds of the air).
- *Ask the creation (please verify):* Job 12:7–10.
- *The earth is for our use and joy (please verify):* D&C 59:16–20.

## 5. Weekly Plan

*Time budget: about 15–20 minutes a day per kid, with one longer outdoor session a week. London in January is dark, cold and wet, so keep sessions short, dress for it, and go anyway. Anything missed in Weeks 1–3 can be caught up in the week after (Feb 1 onwards) before the ceremony.*

**Kickoff weekend (Fri Jan 1 – Sun Jan 3):** see Parent Guide. A New Year family night: share what you want to explore this year.

### Week 1 (Jan 4–10): **Look Closely** (step: Learn)
*Theme: The outdoors is full of things I walk past. When I look closely, I find them.*

**Individual core (each kid does 3):**
- **Monty:** (1) Pick a "sit spot" outside, sit still for 3 minutes (a parent sits too) and count how many living things you can find. (2) With a parent, pick a living thing to look after all month (a bird feeder, a plant, a neighbor's dog) and do one job for it every day. Mark it on a tick chart. (3) Pack your own small bag with a parent: water, snack, hat, gloves, torch. Say what each thing is for.
- **Rudy:** (1) Sit spot for 5 minutes. Draw or write 3 things you noticed and 1 question. Try to answer the question by looking or thinking before you ask. (2) Pick a living thing to look after all month and make a care chart with a parent. Decide what it needs before you ask. (3) Look at a winter-walk packing list, then pack your bag. Say what each item is for before you check the answer.
- **Duke:** (1) Do a 10-minute sit spot three times this week, with a notebook. Notice what changes. (2) Pick a living thing to look after all month (a pet, a neighbor's pet, a plant, or volunteer hours at a city farm if a parent can arrange it) and write a care plan. (3) Build a bag from a "10 essentials" checklist and write why each item is in it. If you can't explain it, take it out.
- **Indiana (pick 3 of 5, her choice):** (a) 20 minutes outside in any weather with a sketchbook or camera. (b) Choose an animal-care commitment for the month (the stable, a city farm, a neighbor's pet, her own, or a project of her own design). (c) Pick a farm animal or breed she's curious about and make a profile (drawing, writing, anything). (d) Read Psalm 19:1-4 and respond in any form. (e) Something of her own.

**Parent tasks:** do your own sit spot, and pick your own living thing to care for.

**Family layer (optional):** start *My Side of the Mountain*. A family "nature scavenger hunt" walk.

**Check-in 1 (Sun Jan 10):** *Look Closely.*

### Week 2 (Jan 11–17): **Be Ready** (step: Practice)
*Theme: Knowing how to pack, tie, cook and help makes me calm instead of scared.*

**Individual core:**
- **Monty:** (1) Learn one knot (a bow for shoes, or a simple overhand knot) and tie it 5 times in a row. (2) Help in the kitchen: wash vegetables and crack an egg or spread butter with a parent beside you. Learn the "hot rule." (3) Put a plaster on a parent or a teddy. Practice shouting for a grown-up and saying your address.
- **Rudy:** (1) Learn a square knot and two half hitches. Tie each 3 times without help. Try first, and look at the steps again before you ask. (2) Cook one simple thing yourself (toast with toppings or scrambled eggs) with a parent standing by. Read all the steps first. (3) First aid: a parent shows you how to clean a scrape, and you practice what to say on an emergency call (pretend).
- **Duke:** (1) Learn a bowline and a clove hitch. Tie each 5 times, then use one for something real (securing a load, a tarp, a rope swing). (2) Cook a simple meal for the family. You plan it, shop for it and cook it. A parent stays close. (3) First aid: learn how to deal with a cut, a burn and a sprain. A parent or a short course can teach it.
- **Indiana (pick 3 of 5):** (a) Learn two useful knots, including a quick-release knot (a stable can show you the safe way for horses). (b) Pack a day kit for her own adventure. (c) Cook or bake something to take on a trip. (d) First aid basics, or ask at the stable what they keep for riders. (e) Something of her own.

**Parent tasks:** learn the same knot your kid is learning, and cook with them.

**Family layer (optional):** a family "kit check": everyone packs a small bag and explains it. Read-aloud continues.

**Check-in 2 (Sun Jan 17):** *Be Ready.*

### Week 3 (Jan 18–24): **Do It Myself** (step: Practice)
*Theme: Real jobs, a little at a time, with a plan. Every step I take on my own earns me more freedom.*

**Individual core:**
- **Monty:** (1) Wet-weather walk: 30 minutes outside in your gear. Find 3 animal signs (a footprint, a feather, a snail trail, a nest). (2) Do one small job by yourself: carry something to someone, or hand over the money at a shop with a parent close by. (3) Draw a map of your street or park with 3 landmarks.
- **Rudy:** (1) Read a map: with a parent, pick a simple park map, find north and 3 landmarks, and plan a 30-minute route. (2) Independence step: do one small errand (ask a shop assistant for something, pay and count your change) with a parent a short distance away. (3) Wet-weather walk, 30 minutes in your gear. Look for animal tracks.
- **Duke:** (1) Learn a map and compass: take a bearing and walk a short route. (2) **Plan your independent journey** in writing: destination, route, times, backup plan (late bus, flat phone), and the safety rules you and a parent agree on. (3) Do a shorter solo trip first (a walk or bus ride to a shop and back).
- **Indiana (pick 3 of 5):** (a) **Plan her own Trailhead:** where, who, how and her rules. (b) Do one independent thing she's been wanting: for example, phone or message the stable herself to ask about group lessons. (c) Read D&C 38:30 or Genesis 2:15 and respond. (d) Photograph or sketch signs of wildlife in the city (foxes, birds, trees). (e) Something of her own.

**Individual core:**

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 3

### Why this month matters
Many children today have less free play and less independence than earlier generations did, and some researchers and authors link that to worse mental health (see sources for how strong that evidence is). This month isn't about thrill-seeking. It's about practicing a staircase of real responsibility: look closely, get ready, do a small thing alone, then lead. Independence grows from doing real things with real (safe) responsibility, and from caring for something that depends on you. You are the biggest factor: you decide when to step back.

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** a child who is nervous about a new step needs a calm adult who believes in them. "This feels scary, and I know you can do it" holds the feeling and the expectation together.

**The fathering idea (The Intentional Father):** high expectation with high support. Believe in them, give them the real job, and stay close enough to help.

**Faith idea:** the earth is God's creation and we are stewards of it and of the living things in our care (Psalm 19:1–4; Genesis 2:15; D&C 38:30 on being prepared).

### Kickoff (Fri Jan 1 – Sun Jan 3)
A short family night (20–30 minutes), New Year style:
1. Explain: "This month is *Wild & Free*. We're going to look closely at the world around us, learn to be ready, do real things on our own, and look after something alive. London in January is dark and wet. We're going anyway. And we're all doing it, including Mom and Dad."
2. **Go first:** share one thing you want to explore this year, and the living thing you'll care for.
3. Introduce The Trailhead (Sat Jan 30) and *My Side of the Mountain*.
4. Show each kid their page in the app. Offer Indiana hers separately and lightly.
5. Show the **gear rack**: set up a hook or box by the door for waterproofs, hats, gloves and a torch.
6. Keep it fun: hot chocolate, a map on the table, a game of "where in the world".

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
The same principles as Months 1 and 2, with a few that matter extra this month:

- **Never use screen time as a reward or a punishment.** Not for finishing a walk, not for skipping one.
- **Never pay per task.** No money for knots learned or miles walked.
- **Do** celebrate with ceremony: the badge ceremony, your outdoor day, the month-end outing.
- **Do** invest in real interests, as support and not payment. For Indiana, a stable visit, a group lesson trial or a farm day. For Duke, a compass or a day-pack. For Rudy, a map or a game with a map. For Monty, a good torch or a pair of wellies. Offer them as support for what they already do, and not as "if you do X then Y".
- **Surprise delight** after the fact: "I saw you tie that knot without asking. Want to go get hot chocolate?"
- **Autonomy is the reward.** When a kid shows they can do a step, the reward is more of the same freedom: a longer route, a bigger errand.
- **Don't compare siblings.** A 6-year-old's 10 minutes and a 13-year-old's journey are different on purpose.
- **Fading:** if a kid heads outside or picks up the rope on their own, scale back your extras.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Be curious. Sit with them and notice out loud, then stop talking. Let the living thing be their responsibility, with a light check from you.
- **Week 2:** Expect "I can't." Let them struggle with the knot for a bit. "What have you tried?" Resist taking over the cooking.
- **Week 3:** This is the hardest week for parents. Stay nearby and quiet. Don't rescue early. Agree on safety first, and then trust it.
- **Week 4:** Let them teach badly. Ask real questions, like someone who doesn't know. Then let them lead the Trailhead.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** What's the best place you've ever been outside? What animal would you most like to meet? What would you take to a desert island?
**Medium:** What's something you can do now that you couldn't do last year? When do you feel most free? What's it like to look after something that can't look after itself?
**Deep:** What's the difference between being brave and being careless? What do you want to be trusted with, and how could you show you're ready? Why do you think God filled the world with so many living things?
**After *The Wild Robot*:** What did Roz learn from the animals? What did the animals learn from Roz? What does it mean to be "wild" and to be part of a family at the same time?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Nature scavenger hunt** (Week 1): a list of things to find on a local walk (a feather, a leaf with a hole, three different birds).
- **Kit check** (Week 2): everyone packs a small bag and explains each item.
- **Wet-weather walk** (Week 3): the whole family, weather be damned. Hot drinks after.
- **London options (check opening times and distances):** a heath or large park, Kew Gardens or the Wetland Centre, a city farm (several exist in London; check before you go), a free museum with an animals or natural history display, a Tube or bus trip with an older kid in charge of the route.
- **Optional hook:** if a ski trip is in the plan, a day on the slopes counts as exploring.

### Your gift: your wild day (Week 4)
Give each child **one outdoor outing with you**, their choice of place and activity, **phone off and in a bag**. No agenda, no teaching, no photos for the sake of photos. Follow their lead and let them navigate. Don't fix their route. At the end, tell them: "I loved exploring with you." Indiana gets an offer, and she picks the day, place and whether you stay quiet. You can spread these across January if four days in one week is too much.

### Per-kid notes
- **Duke:** Fairness matters to him, so explain every safety rule and the reason behind it, and make the rules the same for similar situations. If he disagrees, let him make his case and negotiate where you can. Shyness with new people is the real stretch of his journey. Rehearse the question he'll ask ("Which platform for X?"). He's nearly 14, so this is a good time to extend freedom.
- **Indiana:** An invitation, never an assignment. She'll resist anything that looks like a rule, so offer the menu once, lightly, and let her design the rest. Investments in her interests (a stable day, a farm visit, a trial group lesson) are support for what she already loves. She might not stick with the month and that's fine. The door stays open.
- **Rudy:** He'd rather have help than do it himself, so this month is practice for "try first, then ask". Before you answer, ask "What have you tried?" or "What do you think?" Count each try as a win, whether or not it worked. Expect some big feelings when he gets stuck. "That's frustrating. I'm here. What's your first try?"
- **Monty:** Short, physical, concrete. He'll love the outdoors and wrestling with a rope. His stretch is staying on the plan (not running off) when it's his turn to lead. Give him a clear job with an end ("Lead us to the second lamp post").

### Coach, don't rescue
When they say "I can't" or "I'm lost," resist fixing it. Try: *"That sounds hard. What do you think you could try first?"* Then wait. You can stay close and still hold back. Step in only for safety.

### What to watch for
- **Normal:** nervousness before a first solo step, frustration with a knot or a map, complaints about the cold and wet, "I don't want to go." Most of this passes once they're out.
- **Needs attention:** panic that doesn't ease, a kid who is frightened by being alone, signs of anxiety, or a step that's too big for them. If so, shrink the step. The goal is a stretch, not a fright.
- **Safety:** you decide what is safe for your child and your neighborhood. In England and Wales there is no set legal age for leaving a child alone, but NSPCC advice (as reported in news coverage) is that children under 12 are rarely mature enough for long periods, and under 16 shouldn't be left overnight. Fire, knives, water, roads and strangers need clear rules. Outdoor fires only in permitted places. Check the current NSPCC and TfL pages yourself.
- **Resistance:** a "no" is information. Ask what's behind it.

### If the month falls apart
If you reach Jan 24 with little done, do this and nothing else: **look after one living thing every day, one short wet-weather walk, The Trailhead (shortened is fine), and your outdoor day with each child.** That's the heart of the month.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* · *Research* · *Author's argument* · *Tool*.

#### Claim: "Kids today get less free play and independence, and that may be bad for them"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"A lot of kids today spend less time playing on their own outside than kids used to. Some people who study kids think that makes it harder to grow up confident."*
- **Source:** Peter Gray, *Free to Learn* (2013), and a 2023 paper in the *Journal of Pediatrics* as reported in news coverage, titled "Decline in Independent Activity as a Cause of Decline in Children's Mental Wellbeing." I could not read the full paper. I found the title and summary only through news columns.
- **Label:** Author's argument, with a research review behind it. **Correlational and debated.** Other researchers dispute how much of the rise in teen anxiety and depression comes from less play versus other causes, such as phones. Present it as a serious idea, not settled fact.

#### Claim: "Kids need more independence, free play and responsibility in the real world"
- **Source:** Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024). Haidt's fourth norm is "far more independence, free play, and responsibility in the real world" (per the book's official site, anxiousgeneration.com).
- **Label:** Author's argument, **debated.** Critics say the correlation between phones and mental health doesn't prove causation. This month focuses on the fourth norm.

#### Claim: "Time in nature is good for kids" (Louv's "nature-deficit")
- **Say it to the kids:** *"Lots of people feel better after time outside, and some studies suggest it helps kids focus."*
- **Source:** Richard Louv, *Last Child in the Woods* (2005). Louv coined "nature-deficit disorder." It is **not a medical diagnosis** (not in the DSM-5 or ICD), and Louv himself says so. He cites research such as Kuo and Taylor (2004), *American Journal of Public Health*, on green outdoor activities and ADHD symptoms. I found this reference through a secondary source and did not read the paper.
- **Label:** Author's argument. The idea is influential, but the evidence is **mixed and mostly correlational.** Critics (for example Dickinson, 2013, *Environmental Communication*, as cited by others) argue the claims outrun the data. Say "may help", not "will fix".

#### Claim: "Home alone, bus and Tube rules"
- **Source:** NSPCC guidance on leaving children alone, as reported in UK news coverage (no fixed legal age; under 12 rarely mature enough for long periods; under 16 not overnight). TfL pages on the 5–10 and 11–15 Zip Oyster photocards.
- **Label:** Tool (official guidance). **Check nspcc.org.uk and tfl.gov.uk directly.** The TfL search results were partly old (2008–2010), and I didn't find an explicit rule about when a child may travel alone on the Tube.
