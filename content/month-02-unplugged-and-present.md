## 1. The Big Idea

**Principle:** *My attention is mine to protect. Where I put it shows what I love.*

Last month was about who you are. This month is about what you do with the most valuable thing you have: your attention. Some screens and apps are built to be hard to put down. Kids (and adults) who notice that can choose, and choosing is a form of freedom. Stillness, boredom and being fully present with people are where creativity, calm and relationships grow.

**Four ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **Notice.** Some apps and games are designed to keep me playing. When I notice how, I'm back in charge.
2. **Boredom is where ideas start.** When nothing is planned, my mind gets to wander, invent and rest.
3. **Be present.** The best gift I can give someone is my full attention.
4. **Be still.** Quiet makes room to think, to feel and to listen for God.

**Faith thread (scriptures supporting the principles):**
- *Be still:* Psalm 46:10; D&C 101:16 ("Be still and know that I am God").
- *A still small voice:* 1 Kings 19:11-12.
- *Jesus made space to be alone and pray:* Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16.
- *One thing is needful (being present):* Luke 10:38-42 (Mary and Martha).
- *The Sabbath as a gift:* Exodus 20:8-11; Isaiah 58:13-14.
- *Christmas, pondering:* Luke 2:8-20; Luke 2:19 ("But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart").

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 2

### Why this month matters
Kids spend a large share of their free time on screens, and many of the things on those screens are built to hold attention. This month isn't about shaming screens. It's about practicing the skill of noticing and choosing, and building the habits (boredom, stillness, full attention) that screens crowd out. You are the biggest factor: kids watch what you do.

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** when your child melts down about screens, the feeling is real, and the limit is also real. Stay calm and warm, and don't give in to avoid the feeling. "You can be upset and the answer is still no."

**The fathering idea (The Intentional Father):** undivided attention is how love is felt. The month ends with your gift of one full hour.

### Kickoff (Tue Dec 1)
A short family night (20–30 minutes):
1. Explain: "This month is *Unplugged & Present*. We're going to notice how screens grab us, practice being bored and quiet, and give each other our full attention. And we're all doing it, including Mom and Dad."
2. **Go first:** share your own number or a screen habit you want to change.
3. Introduce the challenge (Unplugged Weekend, Dec 18–20) and *The Phantom Tollbooth*.
4. Show each kid their page in the app. Offer Indiana hers separately and lightly.
5. Agree on **what counts** as an entertainment screen, and the exceptions, so it isn't a negotiation later.
6. Keep it fun: hot chocolate, a board game.

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
The same principles as Month 1, with a few that matter extra this month:

- **Never use screen time as a reward or a punishment this month.** It makes screens more valuable and works against what you're teaching.
- **Do** celebrate with analog rewards: the ceremony, your hour, the month-end outing.
- **Do** invest in screen-free interests: a deck of cards, a board game, art supplies, a ski or riding day. Offer them as support, not payment.
- **Surprise delight** still works: "I noticed you picked up a book instead of the tablet. Want to go get hot chocolate?"
- **Don't compare siblings.** Rudy's weekend and Duke's weekend are different on purpose.
- **Fading:** if a kid reaches for a book, a sketchbook or the yard unprompted, scale back your extras.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Be curious, not judgmental. Say "I noticed I spend X on my phone" before asking about theirs. No lectures.
- **Week 2:** Expect complaints of "I'm bored." That's the point. Resist solving it. Say "I'm curious what you'll come up with" and walk away.
- **Week 3:** Be ready for resistance before the weekend and a hard first few hours. This usually passes. Have your own plan for the weekend too.
- **Week 4:** Keep Christmas week light. Attention is a gift; don't make the plan a chore.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** What's the best game or show you've ever played or watched, and why? What's something you could do for hours without noticing?
**Medium:** Do you think some apps are designed to keep you using them? How do you feel after a long time on a screen? What do people do when they're bored?
**Deep:** Where do you want your attention to go? What do you want to be good at? Who deserves more of your attention than they get?
**After *WALL-E*:** What did the humans miss? What woke them up? What would your life look like if you did everything on a screen?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Family Tech Check** (Week 1): everyone shares numbers, and the family picks one change.
- **Boredom Jar** (Week 2): everyone adds ideas.
- **Sunday stillness hour** (Weeks 2–4): screen-free quiet time.
- **Phone basket:** devices go in a basket at dinner and on Christmas Eve.
- **Walk and wonder:** a short walk with no phones, to notice five things you've never seen.

### Your gift: an undivided hour (Week 4)
Give each child **one hour of your full attention**, their choice of activity, **phone out of sight**. No agenda, no teaching. Follow their lead. Tell them at the end: "That hour was for you."

### Per-kid notes
- **Duke:** Teens are the heaviest users of screens, and 13 is a pivotal age for phones and social media. If he has a phone or will soon get one, this month is a good time to **agree on norms together** (where it sleeps, when it's off) rather than imposing them. Because fairness matters to him, apply the rules to the adults, too, and explain the reasons.
- **Indiana:** Don't frame this as "taking something away." It's her design and her weekend. Ask what she'd do with 24 hours if she could do anything: horses, sketching, skiing, time with a friend are all screen-free. If she says no to the weekend, keep the door open and make the invitation again with no pressure.
- **Rudy:** The gamer in him will find this hardest. Expect a meltdown, and plan for it: a quest log, a friend, a plan for the first hour, which is usually the hardest. Let him complain and stay calm. "I know it's hard. You can do hard things." The **try-first** habit from Month 1 is the tool here.
- **Monty:** He'll probably be the least affected. The challenge for him is stillness: 15 minutes of quiet. Let him wiggle. Make it a game ("Statue"), and don't suggest what to do.

### Coach, don't rescue
When they say "I'm bored" or "I can't," resist fixing it. Try: *"That sounds hard. What do you think you could do?"* Then wait. Don't hand them a screen as the solution.

### What to watch for
- **Normal:** irritability, complaining of boredom, restlessness, moodiness in the first day or two of reduced screen time. This usually passes within a day or so.
- **Needs attention:** intense, prolonged distress that doesn't ease, signs of anxiety or depression, withdrawal from friends, or being unable to function without a device. If that's the case, ease off and talk to someone you trust, such as a pediatrician or counselor.
- **Resistance:** a "no" is information. Ask what's behind it.

### If the month falls apart
If you reach Dec 20 with little done, do this and nothing else: **the Unplugged Weekend (shortened is fine), Christmas Eve together, and your hour with each child.** That's the heart of the month.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* · *Research* · *Author's argument* · *Tool*.

#### Claim: "Kids spend a lot of free time on screens"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"A big survey found that kids your age spend several hours a day on entertainment screens."*
- **Source:** Common Sense Media, *The Common Sense Census: Media Use by Tweens and Teens* (2021). Reported averages were about 5½ hours a day of entertainment screen media for tweens (ages 8–12) and about 8½ hours for teens (13–18), measured during the pandemic. Numbers are as reported in coverage and the organization's articles. Check the report for exact definitions. [Common Sense Media: Tweens, Teens, and Screens](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/tweens-teens-and-screens-what-our-new-research-uncovers).
- **Label:** Research (a survey). The numbers include pandemic years and may be higher than usual.

#### Claim: "Boredom can make people more creative"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"In one experiment, people who did a boring task first came up with more ideas on a creativity test."*
- **Source:** Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman (2014), "Does being bored make us more creative?" *Creativity Research Journal*, 26(2), 165–173. Participants copied phone numbers from a directory, or did a boring reading task, then thought of uses for plastic cups. Open access: [UCLan repository](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/22263).
- **Label:** Research, but small. It's two lab studies with one kind of creativity task, so don't oversell it. Popular articles often exaggerate it. The safest way to say it: *"boredom may give your mind room to wander."*

#### Claim: "A phone on the table drains your brainpower." Be careful.
- **What you may hear:** Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos (2017), "Brain Drain," *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research*, reported that just having your phone nearby reduced thinking capacity.
- **What happened next:** several attempts to repeat the result, including a pre-registered replication (*Acta Psychologica*, 2022) and a partial replication (Hartmann et al., *Consciousness and Cognition*, 2021), **did not find the effect**.
- **Label:** Research, **not reliably supported.** Don't tell the kids that a phone on the table lowers their IQ. If the topic comes up, it's a good example of why scientists try to repeat studies.

#### Claim: "Phone-based childhood has crowded out play and in-person time"
- **Source:** Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024). He argues that a shift from play-based to phone-based childhood has contributed to rising anxiety and depression among teens, and he proposes four norms: delay smartphones, delay social media, phone-free schools, and more independence and free play. The exact ages he suggests should be checked in the book.
- **Label:** Author's argument, **debated.** Some researchers dispute how strong the evidence for causation is. Present it as a serious idea that many parents find useful, not as settled fact.
- **For kids:** *The Amazing Generation: Your Guide to Fun and Freedom in a Screen-Filled World* by Jonathan Haidt and Catherine Price (2025), a guide for roughly ages 9–12.

#### Principle: "Connection comes before correction" (when a kid melts down)
- **Source:** Becky Kennedy, *Good Inside* (2022).
- **Label:** Author's argument (clinical perspective, written for parents).
