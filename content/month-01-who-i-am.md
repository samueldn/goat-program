# Month 1: Who I Am

**Dates:** Nov 8 – Nov 30
Week 1: Nov 8–12 · Week 2: Nov 13–17 · Week 3: Nov 18–24 · Week 4: Nov 25–29 (Madeira trip; Thanksgiving is Thu Nov 26) · Buffer: Nov 30
**Badge:** The Roots Badge
**Check-ins:** Thu Nov 12, Tue Nov 17, Mon Nov 23, Sun Nov 29

## How this month is structured

Every month has two layers:

- **Individual core (earns the badge).** Each kid's own tasks, check-ins with a parent, and challenge. Every item can be done alone with a parent. **No kid's badge depends on a sibling.**
- **Family layer (shared, optional, untracked).** Read-aloud, movie night, family rituals, and the month-end celebration. These build family culture, but nobody's badge depends on them, and skipping them never costs anything.

## 1. The Big Idea

**Mantra:** *Known. Loved. Rooted. Rising.*

*Known by God and by family. Loved without conditions. Rooted in the people we come from. Rising into who we are becoming.*

Identity is **given before it is earned**. Kids who know they are loved and known regardless of performance take braver risks later. Everything in the next 11 months (hard things, discomfort, failure, courage) is safer to attempt from this foundation.

**Three ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **I'm one of a kind.** Nobody has my exact mix of strengths, interests and story.
2. **I'm a good kid, even when I have hard moments.** Strengths and struggles can both be true.
3. **I come from a story.** My family, and God, knew me before I did anything.

**Faith thread (scriptures supporting the principles):**
- *Child of God:* Romans 8:16; "The Family: A Proclamation to the World" ("each is a beloved spirit son or daughter of heavenly parents"); the Primary song "I Am a Child of God."
- *Known by name:* Isaiah 43:1 ("I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine"); John 10:3.
- *Fearfully and wonderfully made:* Psalm 139:13-14.
- *Worth:* D&C 18:10 ("the worth of souls is great in the sight of God").
- *God looks at the heart:* 1 Samuel 16:7.
- *Growing into who you are:* Luke 2:52 (Jesus "increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and man").
- *Roots:* 1 Nephi 1:1 (Nephi begins his story with his family: "I, Nephi, having been born of goodly parents"); Malachi 4:5-6.
- *Gratitude (Thanksgiving week):* Luke 17:11-19 (the one leper who returned to give thanks); D&C 59:7.

*Please check all scripture references against your own copies.*

The default is **Thursday, Nov 26**, at Thanksgiving dinner in Madeira, with family as the audience, but if a kid opts out or a sibling doesn't take part, nobody else is affected. ## 6. Personal Check-ins

**Format:** One-on-one, about 10–15 minutes, somewhere relaxed (a walk, a drive, bedtime, a snack). The kid talks most of the time. You don't fix, teach or correct anything in a check-in. If you want to bring up behavior, do it another time. Use the questions as a menu. One good answer is better than five rushed ones.

The "magnitude" line at the end of each is something to *say*, not ask.

| | Questions (pick 2–3) | Magnitude line |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Known** (Thu Nov 12) | What's something about you that you think most people don't know? If you were a character in a story or game, what would your special power be? What's a part of you that you really like? | *"Out of all the people who have ever lived, there has never been another you. God knew you before you were born."* |
| **2. Strengths and struggles** (Tue Nov 17) | What's something you're good at that you're proud of? What's hard for you right now? Can both of those be true at once? What do you think they'd say about you? | *"You're carrying a story that began long before you were born. A lot of people sacrificed and loved so you could be here."* |
| **4. My story** (Nov 29) | How did it feel to share your story? What are you proudest of from this month? What do you want to remember about who you are? | Read them your letter (below). |

**Kid-adapted tips:**
- **Monty (6):** Keep it short and physical (walk or play while you talk). Ask concrete questions ("What's your superpower?"). Let him draw. He'll answer sideways.
- **Rudy (8):** He's emotional and may cry. That's fine. Let the feeling be there without fixing it. Use the game-character language he loves.
- **Duke (13):** Do it in the car, on a walk, or while shooting hoops. Side-by-side is easier than face-to-face for a shy kid. Don't push; silence is okay.
- **Indiana (12):** Offer it and let her decide where and when, or whether. If she declines, don't push. Say "the offer stands." Her check-in may be one sentence, and that counts.

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 1

### Why this month matters
Everything else in the year (grit, discomfort, failure, courage) asks kids to risk looking foolish or failing. Kids take those risks more willingly when they know they are loved and valued regardless of results. This month builds that foundation.

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** *Separate who your child IS from what they DO.* "You're a good kid having a hard time" is true, and kids need to hear it, especially when they're struggling.

**The fathering idea (The Intentional Father):** Children need to hear, in specific and personal words, what a parent sees in them. This is why the month ends in a letter, not a lecture.

### Kickoff (Sun Nov 8)
A short family night (20–30 minutes):
1. Explain: "We're starting something as a family this year. Each month has a theme, and this month is *Who I Am*. At the end of the year you'll be amazed at what you've done."
2. Share a bit about *you*: a story from your own childhood where you didn't know who you were yet.
3. Introduce the challenge, and *The Wild Robot* if you're doing the read-aloud.
4. Give each kid their menu. For **Indiana**, offer hers separately and lightly (see below).
5. Be clear about how it works: *"Each of you has your own path. Nobody's path depends on anyone else's."*
6. Keep it fun: a treat, a game.

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
Rewards that are promised in advance ("do this, get that") tend to lower a kid's own interest, especially in things they already like. Recognition and surprises don't. So:

- **Do** use ceremony: the badge moment, your letter, the Month 12 reveal.
- **Do** use surprise delight: "I noticed what you did, let's get ice cream." Unannounced.
- **Do** use investments in their interests (a robotics kit, riding time, a new board game), offered as support for something they care about, not payment for tasks.
- **Do** use autonomy as the reward: badge earners can choose a future family dinner or outing.
- **Do** make the month-end celebration unconditional. It marks the month, not the work.
- **Don't** use screen time as currency, pay per task, or withhold your one-on-one time.
- **Don't** compare siblings, or make a leaderboard.
- **The test:** is it a surprise celebration after, or a bribe promised before? Choose the first.
- **Fading:** extras are fine early on. If a kid starts saying "I want to do this," scale them back.

If a kid chooses not to participate in something, there's no punishment. They don't earn that badge, and they're invited back next month.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Be curious, not evaluative. The goal isn't a "right" answer; it's for them to be seen.
- **Week 2:** Model it. Share a strength and a struggle of your own. Kids learn that struggle isn't shameful by watching you admit one.
- **Week 3:** Ask the relatives to tell stories of *struggle*, not just success. Kids need to know that hard things are part of the family's story.
- **Week 4:** Keep Thanksgiving week light. Don't let presentations become performances to be graded. Praise effort and courage, not polish.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** If you could have any animal as a sidekick, which would it be? What's your favorite family tradition?
**Medium:** What makes someone a good friend? Is there anyone in our family you think you're like?
**Deep:** When do you feel most proud of yourself? What would you want people to say about you when you're old? Why do you think God made you the way you are?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Name Night** (Week 1) and **Strengths Jar** (Week 2): described above.
- **Roots wall:** pin photos of ancestors to a wall or fridge during the month.
- **Tradition interview:** each person shares a favorite family tradition and where it came from. It pairs well with Thanksgiving.
- **A "Wild Robot" night:** a short walk outdoors to look for animals or tracks.

### The letter (Week 4)
Write each child a **short, specific, handwritten letter** (half a page). Include:
1. A specific moment this month where you saw something good in them.
2. A strength you see that they may not see in themselves.
3. That you love them, and that nothing they do changes that.

Read it aloud or hand it over after the presentation (Nov 26) or on Nov 29. Don't ask for a response. It may matter more than anything else this month.

### Per-kid notes
- **Duke:** Fairness and shyness are both identity topics. Don't try to "fix" either this month. Let him see that his sense of fairness is a strength. Notice who he chooses to present to, and let him choose a way that stretches but doesn't overwhelm him. Because he cares about fairness, apply the same structure to everyone and explain it.
- **Indiana:** Her version is an invitation, not an assignment. On Nov 8, say something like: *"I'm trying something for our family this year. Your part is totally yours to design. Here's a menu; pick what interests you or make up your own. Nothing is required and I'd love your take."* Then actually let her decline, change or skip pieces. If she engages on her own terms, that's a win. If she doesn't engage at all, it's still worth continuing with the family and leaving the door open. Her horse and acting interests are real connection points; ask about them the way you'd ask about this month.
- **Rudy:** The *Try-First* task is deliberate. When he asks for help, ask: "What have you tried? What do you think the first step is?" Then stay nearby. Don't take over. This is the seed for Month 4.
- **Monty:** He'll love the presenting. The stretch is no parent beside him and answering a question. Let him struggle a little with the answer.

### Coach, don't rescue
When a kid gets stuck, frustrated or says "I can't," resist fixing it. Try: *"That sounds hard. What do you think you could try?"* Then wait. Kids build confidence from solving things themselves.

### What to watch for
- **Healthy struggle:** frustration, hesitation, a few tears, followed by a try. Let it play out.
- **Needs help:** persistent distress, withdrawal, or comments like "I'm worthless" or "nobody likes me." Pause the tasks and talk, and seek help if it continues.
- **Resistance:** this is information. Ask what's behind it before assuming it's defiance.

### If the month falls apart
If you reach the flight on Nov 25 with little done, do this and nothing else: **the letter, and one conversation with each child.** That's the heart of the month.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is. Showing kids how to weigh evidence is part of the lesson. Duke will likely appreciate it.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* (our faith's foundation) · *Research* (a study or set of studies) · *Author's argument* (a well-argued view from a book, not proof) · *Tool* (an instrument you can use).

#### Principle: "Knowing your family's story makes you stronger" (Week 3, Roots)
- **Say it to the kids:** *"Scientists who studied kids found that the ones who knew more about their family's stories, including the hard times, tended to feel more in control of their lives and handled stress better."*
- **Source:** Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush (Emory University), the "Do You Know?" scale. Kids answered about 20 questions on their family history (e.g., where grandparents grew up, how their parents met). Those who knew more tended to have higher self-esteem and a stronger sense of control, and, in follow-up after 9/11, handled stress better. Popularized in Bruce Feiler's New York Times article "The Stories That Bind Us" (2013).
- **Label:** Research, but treat with care. It's a **correlation**: it doesn't prove that learning family stories *causes* resilience, since closer families may do both. Most of what's easy to find about it is press coverage and Emory summaries, so read the original Duke and Fivush papers if you want the specifics. Reading: [Emory: How family stories help children weather hard times](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2020/04/how-family-stories-help-children-weather-hard-times).
- **Kid-friendly honesty:** *"This doesn't prove that family stories make you stronger, but it's a good reason to find out."*

#### Principle: "Everyone has strengths, and you can name yours" (Week 1)
- **Say it to the kids:** *"Scientists have identified 24 character strengths that people everywhere share, like bravery, kindness, curiosity, and fairness. Everyone has a different top few."*
- **Source:** The **VIA Classification of Character Strengths** (Peterson and Seligman, 2004, *Character Strengths and Virtues*), and the **VIA Youth Survey** from the VIA Institute on Character at [viacharacter.org](https://www.viacharacter.org).
- **Label:** Tool, built on research in positive psychology.
- **Practical notes:** The survey is **free** (basic results), takes roughly 10–15 minutes, and has about 96–98 questions. It's for ages **10–17**, but **under 13 needs a parent to register the account**, so register Indiana (12) yourself. Duke (13) can register himself. A longer youth report costs about $10; skip it. The age range and question count vary slightly between pages on their site, so check the registration page.

#### Principle: "Rewards can backfire; recognition helps" (the parent's guide to fuel)
- **Say it to the kids (older ones):** *"Researchers once gave kids who loved to draw a certificate for drawing. Afterward, those kids drew less on their own. But kids who got a surprise reward didn't lose interest."*
- **Source:** Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* ("Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward"), a study of preschoolers drawing with felt-tip pens. Building on that, Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), *Psychological Bulletin*, 125, 627–668, a meta-analysis of 128 studies. They found that **expected, tangible rewards** tended to reduce interest, with larger effects in children than in college students, while **positive feedback** increased it.
- **Label:** Research. Strong and widely cited, but some researchers (Eisenberger, Pierce and Cameron) argue the effect depends on conditions, so I wouldn't say "rewards never work". The takeaway for this program is narrower: *avoid promised, tangible, contingent rewards for things you want them to value; use surprise and recognition.*

#### Principle: "Kids need autonomy, competence and connection" (the design of the whole program)
- **Source:** Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, **Self-Determination Theory**. Overview and research at [selfdeterminationtheory.org](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org).
- **Label:** Research. A major, long-running theory with a large body of evidence.
- **Why it's here:** it's the reason the program gives each kid choices, makes each path independent, and treats Indiana's resistance as a call for autonomy, not defiance.

#### Principle: "A good kid can have a hard moment" (Week 2)
- **Source:** Becky Kennedy, *Good Inside* (2022), her core idea that children are "good inside" and that behavior problems are hard moments, not character flaws.
- **Label:** Author's argument (clinical-psychology perspective, written for parents). It's not an experimental finding.

#### Principle: "Children need to hear what you see in them" (Week 4, the letter)
- **Source:** Intentional-fathering literature, including *The Intentional Father*. **Please add the specific passage you're drawing on:** I'm not confident enough of the book's contents to cite a page or idea.
- **Label:** Author's argument.
