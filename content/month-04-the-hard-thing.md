## 1. The Big Idea

**Principle:** *I choose something hard, practice it a little every day, expect to want to quit, and finish what I start.*

Month 1 was about who you are. Month 2 was about where your attention goes. This month asks what you do with that attention when something is hard and you are not good at it yet. Everyone, parents included, picks one Hard Thing and does a small daily practice for 28 days. The Hard Thing Rule has three parts, adapted from Angela Duckworth's idea for families:

1. **Everyone does a Hard Thing** (parents too), something that needs a bit of practice every day.
2. **You pick it.** Nobody else can choose your Hard Thing for you. It has to be something you are at least a little interested in.
3. **You finish what you start.** You can't quit on a bad day. You can stop at a natural stopping point, and this month that point is the end of the 28 days (Sun Feb 28). Each kid names it on day one.

**Four ideas the month teaches (in kid language):**
1. **Choose it.** A Hard Thing I pick myself is mine, and that makes it worth doing.
2. **Little and often.** Five to fifteen minutes every day beats one big push.
3. **The wobble is normal.** I will want to quit. That is not a sign I'm failing. It's what the middle feels like.
4. **Effort counts twice.** Effort turns talent into skill, and then turns skill into something real I can do.

**Faith thread (scriptures supporting the principles):**
- *I will go and do:* 1 Nephi 3:7. Nephi does hard things because he trusts the Lord will prepare a way.
- *Nephi builds the ship:* 1 Nephi 17:8-18 (told to build a ship) and 1 Nephi 18:1-4 (building it by the Lord's pattern). **Please verify these ranges.** I wrote them from memory.
- *Hard times can teach us:* D&C 122:7-8 (Liberty Jail: "all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good").
- *Do your work with your whole heart:* Colossians 3:23.
- *Do not get weary in well doing:* Galatians 6:9. **Please verify.** Written from memory.

## 7. Parent Guide: Month 4

### Why this month matters
Doing something hard, a little every day, and seeing it through is how kids learn that effort pays off and that wanting to quit is survivable. The evidence on "grit" as a trait is debated (see sources), so the guide treats it as a practice, not a personality score: pick something, practice small, expect the wobble, finish. **You go first.** Your own log, including your bad days, is the most powerful thing you'll do this month.

**The core parenting idea (Good Inside):** when a kid melts down at the wobble, the feeling is real and the commitment is also real. "You can be upset and we're still doing two minutes."

**The fathering idea:** show up. Being present at the finish line tells a child their effort mattered.

### Kickoff (Mon Feb 1)
A short family night (20-30 minutes):
1. Explain: "This month is *The Hard Thing*. Everyone picks something hard, practices it a little every day for 28 days, and finishes. And we're all doing it, including Mom and Dad."
2. **Go first:** share your Hard Thing, your daily practice, and the part you're scared of.
3. Explain the three rules: everyone does one, you pick yours, and you can't quit on a bad day, only at the finish line on Feb 28. Each person says their finish line out loud.
4. Introduce the Finish Line Showing (Feb 27 or 28) and *Wilma Unlimited*.
5. Show each kid their page in the app. Offer Indiana hers separately and lightly.
6. Keep it fun: a favorite dinner and a big sheet of paper for the Hard Thing Wall.

### Fuel and recognition (how to encourage without bribing)
The same principles as earlier months, with a few that matter extra this month:

- **Never use screen time as a reward or a punishment.** Not for finishing the practice, not for missing it.
- **Never pay per rep or per day, and don't promise a prize for finishing.** The Finish Line is its own reward. An announced prize can turn a chosen challenge into a job.
- **Do** use ceremony (the individual badge ceremony) and the non-contingent family celebration.
- **Surprise delight after the fact** still works: "I noticed you practiced on the train. Want a hot chocolate?" Don't announce it beforehand.
- **Do** invest in the real interests behind the Hard Thing, as support and not payment: a riding lesson or group trial for Indiana, a ball or robotics kit for Duke, a swimming session for Monty. Offer it because they care about it, not because they earned it.
- **Autonomy is the reward.** Let them choose the audience, the showing and what comes next.
- **Don't compare siblings.** Each kid's Hard Thing is a different size on purpose.
- **Fading:** if a kid is practicing without prompting, back off and step out of the way.

### Weekly coaching notes
- **Week 1:** Help them choose something hard but not impossible. A good test: they'd say "I'd love to be able to do that" and "I'm not sure I can." Resist choosing for them.
- **Week 2:** Expect the wobble around day 8-14. Stay warm and steady. "You don't have to want to. Just do the two minutes." Don't rescue and don't lecture.
- **Week 3:** Half-term and travel can break routine. Shrink the practice, don't skip it. A 5-minute rep on the road is a win.
- **Week 4:** Plan the showings now, and be there. Don't over-polish. The point is the progress, not a perfect performance.

### Discussion questions (dinner or car; use any)
**Light:** What's something you couldn't do last year that you can do now? Who do you know who's really good at something, and how did they get there?
**Medium:** What makes you want to quit something? What's the difference between quitting and changing your mind? When has "just five more minutes" worked for you?
**Deep:** Why did Nephi say "I will go and do"? What's something hard you'd do even if you were scared? What do you want to be able to say you finished?
**After *Cool Runnings*:** What kept the team going when they were laughed at? What did they do after the crash? What was the hardest part of finishing?

### Family activities (optional)
- **Hard Thing Wall** (Weeks 1-4): everyone's 28-day chart in one place, parents included.
- **Wobble stories** (Week 2): share a time you wanted to quit, parents first.
- **Road practice** (Week 3): everyone does their rep on a trip or outing.
- **Finish line ritual** (Week 4): a ribbon or chalk line everyone crosses.
- **Nephi night** (any week): read 1 Nephi 3:7 and the ship story, and talk about what it means to "go and do."

### Your gift: be at the finish line (Week 4)
Do two things:

1. **Do your own 28-day Hard Thing alongside them,** with a log you share. Include the day you wanted to quit and what you did.
2. **Be there for each kid's showing,** fully present, phone away. Cheer for the effort. At the end say: "I saw how hard you worked. I'm so glad I was here."

### Per-kid notes
- **Monty:** Keep it short and physical. Five to ten minutes of reps is enough, and a colored box on a chart is perfect. When he wants to stop, ask for just two more tries. He may already be good at "pulling it together" outside, so notice when he does that in practice, not just at school. Wrestling at home is energy that could go into his skill: "Want to do ten cartwheels before wrestling?" Don't use it as a deal, only an offer.
- **Rudy:** The try-first ladder is the whole point: try, try a different way, then ask a *specific* question. When he says "can you help me?", reply warmly: "What have you tried?" Then wait. Celebrate steps, not results ("You tried three ways before asking"). He may cry at the wobble. Comfort first, then do the two minutes together. Pair this with his gamer language: it's a boss fight, and bosses take many tries.
- **Duke:** His Hard Thing should have a number, such as a score, a count or a build milestone, so he can see progress. Introduce the plateau early: flat stretches are normal and a sign to change the method, not quit. He's shy with new people, so the showing audience can start small (a friend or coach), with the stretch being to include someone new. Fairness matters to him, so explain the quit rule, invite his view on it, and keep the same rule for the adults. If he disagrees, let him say so and hear him out before holding the line.
- **Indiana:** This is an invitation, not a requirement. Offer the menu, let her design the Hard Thing and the finish line, and let her decide what "quitting" means for her. If she wants to quit something, **don't shame her and don't rescue.** Get curious: Is it the rules or structure? The other people? Boredom? Fear of being bad in front of others? Often it's the last one. Ask: "What would make it feel safer?" Reflect what you hear. Where it fits, invest in her interests as support, such as a trial group riding lesson or a drawing class, not as a reward. If she says no to the month, keep the door open and invite again lightly.

### Coach, don't rescue
When they say "I can't" or "I want to quit," resist fixing it. Try: *"That sounds really hard. What have you tried? What's the smallest piece you could do?"* Then wait. Don't take over, don't lecture, and don't offer a screen as comfort.

### What to watch for
- **Normal:** frustration, tears, bargaining, "I hate this," and wanting to quit around days 8-14. A plateau in progress.
- **Needs attention:** pain or injury (stop and rest), a Hard Thing that has become punishing or taken over their life, signs of anxiety or perfectionism, or tears every day. If so, shrink the goal, change the Hard Thing, or end early, and talk with someone you trust, such as a doctor or counselor. A change in method is not quitting. Changing to a safer or better-fitting Hard Thing with the kid's agreement is allowed.
- **Resistance:** a "no" is information. Ask what's behind it.

### If the month falls apart
If you reach Feb 14 with little done, do this and nothing else: **a smaller Hard Thing (5 minutes a day) for the last two weeks, the Finish Line Showing (even a short one), and your presence at it.** That's the heart of the month. Finishing a smaller thing beats abandoning a big one.

### Where this comes from (sources)

Use these when a kid asks "how do you know that?" Each entry has a way to say it to the kids, the source, and an honest label for how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence labels:** *Scripture* · *Research* · *Author's argument* · *Tool*.

#### Tool: "The Hard Thing Rule"
- **Say it to the kids:** *"There's a rule some families use: everybody does something hard, you pick yours, and you finish what you start."*
- **Source:** Angela Duckworth, *Grit: The Power of Passion and Perseverance* (2016). My searches found sources that agree on three parts (everyone does one, it needs daily deliberate practice, the person picks it), but they **disagree on how the second part is worded**: some summaries say "you must finish what you start" and the book excerpt says "you can quit," with conditions. **Please check the book for the exact wording.** This month's version (no quitting on a bad day; stop at a stated natural stopping point) is my adaptation.
- **Label:** Tool, from an Author's argument. Duckworth's own family practice. I found no research testing the Hard Thing Rule as a program.

#### Claim: "Effort counts twice" (skill = talent x effort, achievement = skill x effort)
- **Source:** Duckworth, *Grit* (2016). This is her model, as given in the brief. **I did not verify the exact formulation in the book through search.**
- **Label:** Author's argument.

#### Claim: "Grit predicts success" Be careful.
- **Source:** Credé, Tynan and Harms (2017), "Much ado about grit: A meta-analytic synthesis of the grit literature," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 113(3), 492-511. It analyzed 584 effect sizes from 88 samples (66,807 people) and reported that grit is only moderately correlated with performance and retention, and very strongly correlated with conscientiousness. Secondary summaries say whether grit adds anything beyond conscientiousness is still debated, and that the "perseverance" part may add more than the "consistency of interest" part. I did not confirm the exact correlations from the paper.
- **Label:** Research, **debated.** Don't tell the kids that grit is a measurable trait that predicts their future. The practice (small daily effort, finishing) is useful whether or not "grit" is a distinct trait. I did not find or verify a study showing that grit-training programs work, so don't claim one does.

#### Claim: "Believing you can improve helps you improve" (growth mindset)
- **Source:** Carol Dweck's mindset work; Sisk et al. (2018), "To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses," *Psychological Science*, 29(4), 549-571. Meta-analysis 1: 273 studies, 365,915 people. Meta-analysis 2 (interventions): 43 studies, 57,155 students. Coverage reports a weak link between mindset and achievement and a small overall effect for school interventions, with some hints of bigger effects for at-risk or low-income students.
- **Label:** Research, **mixed.** Treat mindset as a useful way to talk about effort ("not yet"), not a proven fix. I did not read Dweck's original studies, and the exact effect sizes came from secondary coverage.

#### Claim: "Practice that stretches you works better than just repeating"
- **Source:** Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice research; Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014), "Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis," *Psychological Science*, 25(8). Pooled, deliberate practice explained about 12% of performance differences (r = .35), with more in games (26%), music (21%), and sports (18%), and much less in education (4%) and professions (under 1%). Ericsson's side argued many of the included studies didn't measure deliberate practice the way he defined it, and a reanalysis found larger effects with stricter criteria.
- **Label:** Research, **debated.** Practice matters a lot, but it isn't the whole story. The "10,000 hours" rule is a popular simplification: it's not a magic number and not a guarantee. Don't say it to the kids as a fact.

#### Claim: "Small wins build belief in yourself" (self-efficacy)
- **Source:** Albert Bandura (1977), "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change," *Psychological Review*, 84(2), 191-215. It proposes that belief in your own ability affects whether you start, how much effort you give, and how long you stick with it, and names four sources of that belief, including performance accomplishments (doing it yourself).
- **Label:** Research (an influential theory). I could not confirm from the paper itself that mastery experiences are the "strongest" source, so I've left that out.

#### Principle: "Connection comes before correction"
- **Source:** Becky Kennedy, *Good Inside* (2022).
- **Label:** Author's argument.
